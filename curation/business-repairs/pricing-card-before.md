# The Four-Step Value Conversation

## When to reach for this

A prospective client has a real problem, real budget authority is in the room,
and someone is about to ask what it costs. The instinct is to go away and build
an estimate from hours × rate. This procedure replaces that: it establishes what
the outcome is worth to the client *before* any number is discussed, so the price
is derived from value created rather than effort expended.

Use it on custom, consultative work where the outcome is uncertain and the value
varies wildly by client — brand identity, positioning, web and product design,
strategy engagements. The signal you need it: you're about to guess at a number.

## Do NOT use this when

- **The work is genuinely commoditized.** Fixed-deliverable production with an
  obvious market rate. Value conversations on a $400 logo tweak waste everyone's
  time and read as manipulative.
- **You're not talking to a decision maker.** Running this on a coordinator who
  must relay it upward produces garbage inputs and burns your one chance.
- **The client already sees you as a vendor.** If that hasn't flipped, this
  conversation will fail. Fix the positioning first — see *Inputs required*.
- **You're the incumbent on a renewal** with an established rate. Different play.
- **Public-sector or procurement-driven RFPs** where price is submitted blind
  against a scoring rubric. The mechanism doesn't allow it.

## Inputs required

Three conditions must hold *before* step 1. If any is missing, stop and fix it —
proceeding anyway is the single most common reason this fails.

1. **The client sees you as an expert practitioner, not a vendor.** The tell is
   the moment they stop talking and start taking notes, or defer to you on
   process ("what do we do next?"). Vendors get their price dictated to them
   because a vendor is assumed to be replaceable.
2. **You already know their desired future state** — surfaced in an earlier
   qualifying conversation, not improvised here.
3. **Senior decision makers responsible for future value are present.** Not
   procurement. Not a project coordinator.

If #2 is missing, you can recover inside the conversation with the three-year
question (step 1). If #1 or #3 is missing, reschedule.

## Procedure

### Step 1 — Commit the client to their desired future state

If you haven't already surfaced it, ask the three-year question: *it's three
years from now, we're having coffee, you're happy with the progress — what
happened?* Let it run. Personal wants surface alongside corporate ones, and the
personal ones matter.

Then recap what you heard, in their language, as a list. Close with two
questions: **"Is that correct?"** and **"Did I miss anything?"**

You are demonstrating three things: you listened, you missed nothing, and this
conversation is about them.

*Judgment call:* don't repeat back personal disclosures (career hopes riding on
the project) in front of colleagues. Hold those privately.

### Step 2 — Determine the metrics of success

Convert each item on the list into something measurable. Walk down the list one
item at a time: *how will we know when we've achieved this?*

For soft objectives, ask for a benchmark rather than a metric — "what's the
maximum percentage of your time you'd want this to require?" Most client-side
marketers want metrics; they need to prove their own value internally. This step
reads as partnership, not interrogation.

### Step 3 — Agree on the value of success

For each metric, ask: **"What is the value of achieving this?"**

Do the arithmetic out loud and confirm it. If they say margin moves 11% → 18% on
$20m, and revenue targets $25m: *"So that's $1.4m on existing sales, plus $900k
on the new $5m at 18% — about $2.3m a year in additional profit. Correct?"*

Push revenue figures down to profit where you can. Quantify the soft items where
it doesn't feel forced, then stop — over-pushing on things people don't price
(time with family, reputation, relief) reads as mercenary and can lose the room.

Sum it up, then round *down* for uncertainty and call it a floor: "$3m+."

### Step 4 — Offer pricing guidance

Do not leave without giving a range. Tell them you'll return with several
different ways you could help, ranging from $X to $Y.

**Say the high number first.** You contribute $Y; let the client's reaction
contribute $X. This foreshadows the proposal so nothing is a surprise, keeps the
frame on return rather than expense, and — most usefully — harvests their
willingness to pay before you've committed to anything.

To find $Y, ask yourself: *what would we charge, and do, if this client had
unlimited resources?* Treat it as genuinely hypothetical, especially if you know
them to be frugal — the exercise exists to detach your number from your own
assumptions about their budget.

### Then: build three or four options, never one

- **Option 1** lands within their stated budget or expectation.
- **Option 2** is the high-margin option you actually want to sell.
- **Option 3** is the anchor. Its job is not to be bought. Its job is to make
  option 2 look reasonable.

## Decision rules

- **Never present a single price.** One price is a yes/no referendum on you.
  Three prices change the question from *whether* to *which* — the client is
  choosing among your options instead of choosing between you and someone else.
- **The high number goes first, always.** The first figure heard sets the frame
  for every figure after it.
- **If value can't be quantified at all, price on the anchor exercise alone**
  rather than falling back to hours × rate.
- **If they name a budget before step 3,** don't accept it as the ceiling.
  Continue the value work; the budget was set before they understood the upside.
- **If value lands below your minimum viable engagement,** decline or reshape the
  scope. Discovering this in step 3 is the procedure working, not failing.
- **Options need not all sit inside their stated budget.** There's no penalty for
  an anchor priced well above it.

## Failure modes

- **Skipping to price because the conversation feels awkward.** The discomfort is
  the work. Bailing to "I'll send an estimate" forfeits the entire method and
  puts you back in a rate comparison.
- **Running it without The Flip.** If they still see a vendor, every value
  question reads as a setup for a markup. Cause: skipping the probative and
  qualifying conversations. Correction: don't hold the value conversation yet.
- **Over-quantifying emotional value.** Pushing someone to put a dollar figure on
  peace of mind alienates them. Push slightly past your comfort level, then stop.
- **Anchoring against your own fear.** Setting $Y at what you think they'll pay
  rather than what unlimited resources would buy. The anchor is hypothetical —
  it isn't meant to be purchased, so your discomfort is irrelevant.
- **Presenting options that are just tiers of the same thing.** Small/medium/large
  of one deliverable invites line-item negotiation. Options should represent
  genuinely different ways of solving the problem, with different scopes of value.
- **Quantifying value, then pricing off cost anyway.** Very common. You run all
  four steps, establish $2.3m of value, and then quote hours × rate out of habit.
  Write the anchor down *before* you open the estimating spreadsheet.
- **Abandoning the method after one bad attempt.** This is a skill built across
  conversations. Most people quit after one or two failures.

## Worked micro-example

Regional retailer wants a brand identity refresh. Instinct says "identity system,
~$40k, that's what we charge."

Run the procedure instead. Three-year question surfaces: move upmarket, stop
competing on price, open two flagship locations, and — personally — the marketing
director wants to stop defending the brand budget to her CFO every quarter.

Metrics: average transaction value from $38 → $55; gross margin +6 points; two
locations open within 24 months.

Value: on $12m revenue, six margin points is $720k a year. She confirms it. The
CFO problem is real but she won't price it, so leave it — noted, not quantified.

Guidance: *"We'll come back with three ways to approach this. They'll range from
around $250k down to something closer to $60k."* Her reaction to $250k tells you
where the real ceiling is, before you've committed to anything.

Proposal: $60k identity system / $145k identity + retail environment + launch /
$250k the full programme including the internal story and CFO-facing measurement
dashboard. Option 2 sells. The original instinct was $40k.
