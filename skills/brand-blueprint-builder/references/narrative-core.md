# Narrative Core — Full Section 2 Walkthrough

The narrative core is the abstract layer above tactics. Most brands skip this work and pay for it later — every campaign, hire, and strategic decision feels arbitrary because the brand never anchored its "why."

This walkthrough produces 12 outputs in order. Earlier outputs feed later ones.

---

## 1. Purpose — the WHY

**One sentence.** The reason the brand exists beyond making money. If the brand made enough money to never work again, what would it still be doing?

Failure modes:
- Too vague: "We help businesses succeed." (everyone says this)
- Too tactical: "We sell software to sales teams." (that's what you do, not why)
- Too aspirational: "We want to change the world." (without specifying how)

**Test:** the purpose, read aloud, should make a non-customer say "OK, that's a real thing." Not "OK, that's marketing."

**Examples (good):**
- Patagonia: "We're in business to save our home planet."
- Stripe: "Increase the GDP of the internet."
- Buffer: "Help people build their audience and business."

---

## 2. Mission — the WHAT

**Two sentences max.** What we do, for whom, and how we measure it.

```
Sentence 1: WHAT — the action and the audience.
Sentence 2: HOW WE MEASURE IT — the explicit metric or signal.
```

**Test:** if you removed all adjectives, the mission should still survive. Mission is operational, not poetic.

---

## 3. Vision — the WHERE

**One paragraph in present tense, as if it's already here.** What does the world look like if the brand succeeds? Not "we will" — "we are" / "they are" / "it is."

The present tense is the discipline. Vision in future tense ("we will become...") stays aspirational forever. Present tense forces specificity.

**Examples (structure):**
- "Every {target customer} can {capability the brand makes possible}. They no longer {old pain}. They now {new state}. {Industry or world} is {how it's different}."

---

## 4. Values — typically 5

Each value gets a **full treatment**, not a one-liner. The full treatment is the work — anyone can list values; few do them justice.

Per-value structure:

```markdown
### Value name (3 words max — make it memorable)

**Definition (one line):**
What this value means in this brand's context.

**Why this value (one paragraph):**
Why this value, here, now. What problem in the industry / category / world does it answer?

**Behaviors that make it visible (3):**
- Specific behavior the brand exhibits in {context}
- Specific behavior the brand exhibits in {context}
- Specific behavior the brand exhibits in {context}

**Anti-behaviors (what violates it) (3):**
- Specific behavior the brand refuses, even when convenient
- Specific behavior the brand refuses, even when convenient
- Specific behavior the brand refuses, even when convenient

**Example moment:**
A real (or representative) story where this value was tested and held.
```

**Common mistake:** values that are universal goods. "Excellence." "Integrity." "Innovation." Every brand claims these, so they signal nothing. Better values are *contrarian* — they're values the brand holds that direct competitors do NOT.

**Test:** for each value, you should be able to name a competitor that doesn't share it. If you can't, the value is universal — pick a sharper one.

---

## 5. Principles — how we decide

Principles are derived from values + experience. They're operational decision rules, not aspirations.

**Format:** "When {situation}, we {decision rule}, because {value being protected}."

5–10 principles. Each principle should answer a real decision the brand makes regularly.

Examples:
- "When a client asks for work outside our core competency, we refer them to a trusted partner, because *built by the hand that designs it* — we don't subcontract."
- "When a campaign gets engagement but no conversions, we kill it next week, because the *North Star* is qualified pipeline, not impressions."

---

## 6. Promise — what every client experiences

**One sentence.** The "if-this-isn't-true-the-brand-is-broken" commitment. The thing the brand stakes its reputation on.

**Test:** if the promise is broken, the brand is wronged. The promise is the contract.

Examples:
- A consulting brand: "Every engagement produces a deliverable I'd put my name on."
- A SaaS brand: "Every customer gets a working integration in 48 hours or we refund."

---

## 7. Anti-vision — what we refuse to become

**3–5 specific brands or archetypes the brand will never imitate.** This is harder than vision because it requires the brand to name competitors / patterns it disrespects.

The anti-vision protects the brand from drift. Without it, brands end up in the middle of the market (because the middle feels safe) and become indistinguishable.

Format:
```markdown
- {Specific brand or archetype}: {what we refuse about it} — {why}
```

---

## 8. North Star Metric

**One number.** If this metric grows, the brand is healthy. If it stalls, something is wrong even if other metrics look good.

**Test:** the North Star Metric should NOT be a vanity metric (followers, downloads, page views). It should be a value-delivered metric — something that only grows when customers get value.

Examples:
- Slack: daily messages sent (proxy for daily active engagement)
- Stripe: transaction volume processed
- A consulting brand: number of locked engagements per quarter

---

## 9. Elevator pitches — four lengths

**10-second** (the headline): one sentence. Used in chance encounters, podcast intros, social bios.

**30-second** (the networking): 60 words. Used at conferences, intros, pitches.

**60-second** (the podcast intro): 120 words. Used as the host's intro, panel intro, longer email intro.

**2-minute** (the speaker / panel version): 240 words. Used as the speaker bio for keynotes or as the "about" section in a comprehensive bio.

Each length is NOT just a longer version of the previous. Each length has a different purpose:
- 10s: hook — earn the next 30 seconds
- 30s: position — name the category and the differentiation
- 60s: prove — cite one specific outcome or signature project
- 2-min: contextualize — provenance, mission, what's next

---

## 10. Manifesto — the rallying declaration

**200–400 words.** The thing that lives on the About page or in the speaker intro or as a pinned post. The manifesto is the brand's loudest external voice.

Structure:
- **Opening line** — the hook. Often a contradiction or call-out of an industry pattern.
- **The problem** — what's broken in the world / industry / category.
- **The thesis** — what the brand believes is true that others miss.
- **The promise / commitment** — what the brand will do about it.
- **Closing line** — a memorable phrase, often the brand's tagline.

The manifesto is the place to use the *primary archetype's* voice loudly. A Sage manifesto teaches. A Creator manifesto declares. A Rebel manifesto calls out. Match the form to the archetype.

---

## 11. Positioning Statement

**One sentence in this form:**
> "For {audience}, {brand} is the {category} that {unique thing} so that {outcome}."

This is the most-tested formula in brand strategy (Geoffrey Moore lineage). Every word matters.

- **Audience** — specific. Not "businesses." "B2B SaaS founders at $1M-$10M ARR with no marketing leader."
- **Category** — the bucket the customer puts you in. Not invented; named.
- **Unique thing** — the differentiation. Not a feature; an approach or a stance.
- **Outcome** — what the customer gets that they couldn't get elsewhere.

---

## 12. Tagline hierarchy

**Primary tagline** — one line that pairs with the logo on the homepage, signature, deck cover.

**Alternates (2–3)** — for different contexts:
- Internal (for the team): "Strategy. Systems. Scale."
- Speaking / events: "Built by the hand that designs it."
- Social bios: a punchier, shorter variant.

The hierarchy prevents the brand from drifting to whatever tagline the latest copywriter felt like writing.

---

## Review discipline

Re-walk this section at every major brand review (annually minimum, or after any major pivot). Most items lock for 2-3 years. The North Star Metric reviews quarterly. The manifesto is permanent — if it changes, the brand has fundamentally changed.
